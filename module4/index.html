Lecture 4.1 · slides 3–4 · Motivation: beyond geometry

Same shape, different material

LikeA chocolate bunny and a plaster bunny come out of the same mold. You still never mix them up.

Every sphere here has identical geometry and identical light. Only the material numbers change: ambient, diffuse, specular color and shininess, taken from the Learn OpenGL material table your slide uses. Pick a material and move the light.

In one lineGeometry gives the outline. The material numbers give the look.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 5 · Lambertian model

Lambert: matte reflection

LikeA solar panel collects the most when it faces the sun squarely.

A matte leaf scatters light equally in every direction. Its brightness depends only on how squarely it faces the sun, the n·l term. Move the sun and tilt the leaf.

In one lineMatte brightness = albedo × cos(angle to the light), and it looks the same from every camera.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 5 · Phong model

Phong: the moving highlight

LikeThe sun glint on a car windshield slides along as you walk past.

A waxy leaf also reflects like a weak mirror, so a bright spot appears near the mirror direction and moves with the viewer. The exponent s sets how tight it is: rubber is wide, polished plastic is tight.

In one lineHighlight = ks·cosˢ(angle from mirror). Bigger s, tighter spot.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 5 · Cook-Torrance model

Cook-Torrance: microfacets and Fresnel

LikeCalm water mirrors the sun sharply. Ripples smear it into a wide glitter path, and the glare is strongest looking across the lake.

The surface is treated as millions of tiny mirrors. Roughness α sets how much their tilts vary, which sets the highlight width (the D term). Fresnel (F) says every surface reflects more at grazing angles.

In one lineRoughness sets the width, F₀ sets the strength, and everything glares at grazing angles.

Lecture 4.1 · slides 6 and 8 · Neural reflectance models, practical considerations

Learning reflectance from views

LikeTasting soup with a few spoonfuls: if you never dip where the spice settled, you miss it.

A neural reflectance model is not given Phong. It learns brightness-versus-view from photos. Here a small learned model (a sum of lobes with fitted weights) sees only the camera views you allow. Few views, and it usually misses the highlight. This is the 'needs extensive data' point on slide 8.

In one lineA learned material is only as good as the view directions it was shown.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 6 · NeRFactor: albedo

Taking a photo apart: albedo × shading

LikeA white shirt in shade and a gray shirt in sun can give the same pixel.

Pixel = the leaf's own color (albedo) × the light it got (shading). One photo cannot separate them: double one and halve the other. The Retinex rule guesses that sharp jumps are paint and slow changes are light. It works until a shadow has a sharp edge.

In one lineOne photo has two unknowns per pixel, so the split always needs an extra assumption or more photos.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 6 · NeRFactor: normals

Normals from several lights: photometric stereo

LikeSweeping a flashlight across a coin to read its raised letters.

Keep the camera fixed and switch lights on one at a time. Each light gives one equation I = ρ·(n·l). Enough lights solve for the normal n and the albedo ρ. Bunched lights let noise win.

In one lineSpread-out lights work like a wide stereo baseline: they make the equations well conditioned.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 6 · NeRFactor: visibility

Visibility: who sees the sun and the sky

LikeStanding under a tree: are you in a sun fleck, and how much open sky is above you?

Visibility asks, for each point and direction, whether a ray escapes. Under a canopy, a soil point is either in a sun fleck or in shadow, and it sees only part of the sky.

In one lineThe sun gives on/off shadows; the sky gives a smooth fraction.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 6 · NeRFactor: illumination

Lighting in a few numbers

LikeA matte leaf hears only the bass notes of the sky, so a few low notes are enough to describe what it sees.

Store the whole sky as a sum of waves (harmonics). A sharp sun needs many waves. A matte leaf adds light over half the sky with a cos weight, which blurs away fast waves, so its shading needs very few. Real systems use 9 spherical-harmonic numbers per color.

In one lineMatte shading is a blurred sky, so about 5 numbers (2D) or 9 (3D) describe it.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 7 · Free-viewpoint relighting

Relighting: same scene, new sun

LikeA stage set is built once. The lighting crew moves the lights later.

The plot was captured with the sun at 50°. A plain NeRF stores the colors it saw, shadows included. A factored model stores albedo, normals and visibility, so it can recompute shading for any sun.

In one lineRelit image = albedo × shading for the new light. A plain NeRF cannot do this.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 7 · Material editing; slide 9 · use cases

Material editing

LikeA car configurator repaints the car without rebuilding it.

With the scene factored, change the leaves' albedo and shine and keep everything else. That is material editing: a virtual product showcase, or a 'what if this plot yellowed?' preview. Shadows and shape stay put because only the material map changed.

In one lineChange one factor and re-multiply: new material, same light, same shape.

Lecture 4.1 · slide 9 · use cases (agriculture)

Why relight field scans: sun by the hour

LikeWalking diagonally across a crowded room, you bump into more people than walking straight across.

Beer-Lambert light interception with a moving sun. A low sun crosses more leaves on its way down (path ∝ 1/sin e), so lower leaves get less light. Two plots scanned at 7 am and noon differ partly because of the clock.

In one lineCompare field scans at the same light, or relight them first.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 4 · The bottleneck: speed

The bottleneck: time per frame

LikeA flipbook: every page must be drawn in 16.7 ms to look smooth at 60 fps.

A frame costs pixels × work per pixel. A classic NeRF asks a big network at every sample on every ray. Instant-NGP asks a tiny one. MobileNeRF runs one tiny network per pixel. Splatting blends a few dozen splats per pixel. Rough counts at 30% of peak chip speed.

In one lineSpeed is set by how many times you ask a network per pixel.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 4 · The bottleneck: memory

The bottleneck: memory

LikePacking a carry-on with a weight limit.

Each method stores something different: network weights, hash tables, or a list of Gaussians. A Gaussian holds position 3, scale 3, rotation 4, opacity 1 and color 3·(deg+1)². Compare with a 50 MB field download.

In one lineNeRF is small and slow; splats are big and fast.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 5 · Hash encoding (Instant-NGP)

Multi-resolution hash encoding

LikeA coat check with fewer hooks than coats: some coats share a hook, and it mostly works.

Features live on grids at several resolutions and are added up. Coarse levels get their own slot per vertex. Fine levels have more vertices than the table has slots, so a hash makes them share. Here the 'network' is just the sum, fit by least squares.

In one lineMany resolutions in a small table: fast lookups replace most of the big network.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 6 · Pruning and quantization

Pruning and quantization

LikeRounding prices to the nearest dime, and dropping the pennies entirely.

Quantization stores each number with fewer bits. Pruning drops the smallest numbers. Both shrink the file. Find where the error curve turns up.

In one line8 bits is nearly free; below about 4 bits quality falls off a cliff.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 6 · Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation

LikeA student who studies the teacher's worked answers learns more than one who sees only a few noisy answer keys.

The teacher is the big hash-grid model (192 numbers). The student is a small model (K bumps). Train the student on a few noisy real measurements, or on many answers queried from the teacher. Same student, very different result.

In one lineA small model learns best from a big model's dense, clean answers.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 7 · Efficient Gaussian splatting

Splatting: stop when the pixel is full

LikeStacking tinted glass panes: after a few, no light gets through, so you stop stacking.

Splats are sorted front to back and blended with C = Σ Tᵢαᵢcᵢ. Once the light left over, T, drops below 1/255, nothing behind can change an 8-bit pixel. With opaque leaves that is after a handful of splats.

In one lineNo network, and most splats are never touched: that is why splatting is real time.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 7 · Gaussian splatting: color cost

View-dependent color costs numbers

LikePlaying a sharp click on a bass-only speaker: it comes out as a soft thump.

A waxy leaf changes color with the view. Splats store that as harmonics in the view direction. A tight highlight needs high orders, and every order adds numbers to each Gaussian.

In one lineLow orders blur highlights; high orders cost memory. Degree 3 is 48 of a splat's 59 numbers.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 8 · MobileNeRF

MobileNeRF: triangles, then a tiny network

LikePaint-by-number: draw the shapes fast, then a simple rule colors each cell.

(a) Triangles drawn by the phone's normal graphics pipeline. (b) A texture stores features instead of colors. (c) Rasterizing gives a feature image. (d) A tiny network turns each pixel's features plus view direction into color: one call per pixel. The weights here are made up; MobileNeRF trains them.

In one lineRasterize first, then one small network call per pixel instead of dozens per ray.

Lecture 4.2 · slides 9 and 11 · Edge deployment, trade-offs

Size against quality: the frontier

LikePicking a phone photo setting: file size against picture quality. The good choices sit on the edge.

Every dot is one compression setting of the same hash-grid model (bits × pruning). The green dots form the frontier: no other setting is both smaller and better. Set the device budget and see the best quality you can afford.

In one linePick from the frontier; any setting below it wastes bytes or quality.

Lecture 4.2 · slide 10 · Benchmarks and metrics

How do we score a render? PSNR and SSIM

LikeTwo photos with the same error score, one grainy and one blurry, look different to people.

PSNR measures average pixel error in decibels: higher is better. SSIM compares local structure: brightness, contrast and pattern. A uniform brightness shift gets a poor PSNR but almost perfect SSIM. Benchmarks report several metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, fps, size) for this reason.

In one lineReport several metrics: PSNR counts pixel error, SSIM looks at structure.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 5 · AR object placement and occlusion

AR occlusion: in front or behind?

LikeAn AR sticker that must hide behind the real leaf.

AR draws a virtual tag only where it is nearer than the real scene: a depth test against your reconstruction. Noise and blurred edges make the tag poke through leaves or vanish in front of them.

In one lineAR needs good depth exactly where surfaces are close to the virtual object.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 6 · Gaming and dynamic scenes: latency

Latency: the picture arrives late

LikeA laggy video call: by the time the image arrives, you have already moved.

While a frame renders, the head or camera keeps turning. The image lands late by (turn speed × latency). VR is commonly targeted at about 20 ms motion-to-photon. Compare render methods using the frame times from the bottleneck concept.

In one lineLag = turn speed × latency. Only real-time methods keep it under a few pixels.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 7 · Scientific and educational visualization

Slice a neural field: a CT scan for plants

LikeA CT scan: one slice at a time through the inside of the object.

A neural field can be asked for density anywhere, so you can slice it like a CT scan. Here is a maize stalk cross-section: dense rind, soft pith, vascular bundles. Move the slice and measure stalk width and rind thickness from where the density crosses a threshold.

In one lineQuery the field anywhere: slicing turns a 3D model into measurements.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 8 · Multimodal integration: LiDAR

Images + LiDAR: trust each where it is good

LikeTwo witnesses: one has sharp eyes up close, the other is steady at every distance. You weigh each by how reliable they are.

Stereo depth error grows as Z²·Δd/(f·B). LiDAR error stays about constant. Fusing with inverse-variance weights gives an error smaller than either, and LiDAR also fixes the scale that photos alone cannot.

In one lineWeight each sensor by 1/error²; the fused depth beats both.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 8 · Multimodal integration: hyperspectral NeRF

Hyperspectral: NDVI from albedo, not raw pixels

LikeJudging fruit ripeness under a yellow streetlight instead of daylight.

Healthy leaves reflect little red and a lot of near-infrared. NDVI = (NIR − R)/(NIR + R). The ratio cancels plain brightness, but not haze or shade light that is richer in NIR. Computing NDVI from albedo fixes both.

In one lineStore reflectance per band; health indices then stop changing with shadows.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 9 · Dynamic NeRFs (Nerfies, scene flow)

Dynamic scenes: wind

LikeA long-exposure photo of a waving hand turns into a blur; a camera that tracks the hand keeps it sharp.

A leaf tip swings during capture. A static model must explain every frame with one shape, so it averages into a faint smear. A deformable model keeps one sharp canonical leaf plus a shift per frame.

In one lineSeparate what the leaf is from where it is now.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 9 · Generalization

Generalization: a prior from many plants

LikeA sketch artist who has drawn thousands of faces needs only a few clues to draw yours.

From scratch, a model needs many views of each new plant. A model trained on 300 plants learns their common shapes (principal components). A new plant then needs only a few measurements to pin down a few numbers.

In one lineA learned prior trades many views per scene for a few numbers per scene.

Lecture 4.3 · slide 10 · Research frontiers: 4D and generative

Frontiers: generate plants, model growth in 4D

LikeA recipe instead of a photo album: store how to make it, not every picture of it.

Generative: draw random numbers for the prior's shapes and you get new, plausible plants nobody scanned. 4D: store one canonical plant plus a small change per time step instead of a full model per day.

In one lineLearned priors let models create and track change, not just copy one scene.
